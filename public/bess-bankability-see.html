How to Assess the Bankability of a BESS Project in Southeast Europe (2026)

How I have come to think about what makes a battery storage project financeable in Southeast Europe, from the contract and risk side

Elevat Solar Consulting · September 2026 · 7 min read

I come to battery storage from the contract and risk side, not the engineering bench. And the longer I work around these projects, the clearer one thing becomes: bankability is where most storage projects are actually won or lost. The technology usually works. What decides whether a project gets financed is how the risk is structured, allocated and protected. That is the lens I use here, and it happens to be the same lens lenders use.

Some context first. Battery energy storage has become the fastest-growing energy asset in Southeast Europe. Bulgaria alone passed its 2030 storage target four years early and now has one of the highest shares of storage relative to grid size in the world. Romania has entered Europe's top ten. Grant money, EU recovery funds and well-designed auctions have kick-started a market that barely existed three years ago.

But building a battery and financing one are two different things. As the sector matures, the question that decides which projects get built and which stall at the investment committee is no longer how big is the pipeline. It is: is this project bankable?

What follows is how I break down what bankability actually means for a BESS project in Southeast Europe in 2026, and how to assess it before you commit capital.

What bankability really means

Bankability is not a single test. It is a stack of assessments that must all resolve at the same time. A project can have first-class equipment, a strong EPC and a good site, and still fail to reach financial close if the revenue structure does not convince a lender. Conversely, a modest project with a solid contracted revenue stream can be financed comfortably.

At its core, bankability is the answer to one question a lender asks: will this project generate enough predictable cash flow, over the life of the loan, to repay the debt with margin to spare, even if things go wrong?

Everything below feeds into that single question.

The revenue stack, and why it is the heart of the matter

A BESS project earns money from several sources, stacked together. Understanding this stack is the foundation of any bankability assessment.

Energy arbitrage. Buy electricity when it is cheap, usually midday when solar floods the market, and sell it when it is expensive, usually the evening peak. This is the simplest revenue source and the one most SEE projects were built around.

Ancillary services. Payments from the grid operator for keeping the system stable, frequency regulation, reserves, balancing. These can be lucrative early, but they saturate quickly as more batteries compete for the same limited market.

Capacity payments. Where a capacity market exists, the battery is paid simply for being available. Italy's MACSE auction and capacity mechanisms in Poland and Belgium provide this. Most of the Western Balkans do not yet have one.

The critical point for 2026: the revenue mix is shifting under everyone's feet. Across Europe, revenue per MW ranges from around 40 to 65 thousand euro per year in low-opportunity markets to over 105 to 160 thousand in high-value ones. Sophisticated multi-market optimisation can add 30 to 50 percent on top. But the simple arbitrage model that built the SEE market is exactly the model getting harder, because as everyone builds batteries to capture the same daily price swing, the swing itself narrows.

The merchant risk problem

Here is the central tension in BESS financing today.

Lenders want predictable, contracted revenue they can write a loan against. But most BESS revenue is merchant, meaning it depends on volatile market prices that no one can guarantee. This mismatch is the single biggest obstacle to BESS bankability in 2026.

A fully merchant project, earning purely from market prices, is the highest risk and hardest to finance with debt. It is often built with mostly equity, which is expensive. The market has responded with contract structures that convert merchant volatility into something a lender can accept.

Tolling agreement. A creditworthy offtaker pays the owner a fixed fee for the right to operate the battery and take its market revenue. The owner gets predictable cash flow regardless of market performance. This is the most bankable structure, and its rise is what allowed BESS to reach solar-scale investment volumes.

Revenue floor. The offtaker guarantees a minimum revenue, protecting the downside, while the owner keeps a share of the upside. Less protective than a toll, but preserves more potential return.

Fully merchant. The owner keeps all risk and all reward. Highest potential return, hardest to finance.

For SEE specifically, tolling and floor structures are still less available than in the UK or Germany, which makes the next factor decisive.

The role of public banks and EU money

In Southeast Europe, one factor converts more speculative projects into bankable ones than any other: public co-financing.

EIB and EBRD guarantees, InvestEU frameworks, first-loss structures and EU Recovery and Resilience funds are what make the difference in Bulgaria, Romania and across the Balkans. Where a commercial lender will not carry merchant risk alone, a public guarantee or grant reduces that risk to a level private capital will match.

Any serious bankability assessment in the region must map which public instruments the project can access. A project structured to use them is materially more financeable than an identical project that ignores them.

Cannibalisation, the risk most models underestimate

This is where regional experience matters, and where many financial models are quietly too optimistic.

As solar and storage flood the same market, midday prices collapse and the arbitrage spread narrows. In Bulgaria, the average midday price fell by more than 23 euro per MWh through 2026, and negative-price hours rose from 11 in 2023 to over 160 in the first seven months of 2026 alone.

A bankability model built on the price spreads of 2024 and 2025 will overstate revenue for a project operating in 2027 and beyond. Lenders are now stress-testing merchant exposure far more conservatively than a year ago, and they are right to. Any credible assessment must model a narrowing spread, not project today's spread forward unchanged.

The technical and delivery layer

Financial structure is only half of bankability. The other half is whether the asset will physically perform and survive.

Equipment bankability. Is the battery from a manufacturer with a real track record, financial strength, certifications and regional service capability? BloombergNEF's Tier 1 list is the common signal, but it is not sufficient on its own.

EPC and delivery. Can the contractor actually deliver on time and on budget? A commissioning delay before grid connection postpones the start of revenue while debt obligations have already begun.

Insurance. Increasingly central, not an afterthought. A damaged transformer in transit or a fire during testing can delay revenue for months. Lenders now expect a credible insurance concept early, in the planning phase, not at financial close.

Grid connection. The connection point, export limit and timeline are fundamental. In SEE, grid capacity and connection queues are a growing constraint, and a project's position in that queue is a bankability factor in its own right.

A bankability checklist for SEE

Before committing capital to a BESS project in the region, work through these questions:

Is the revenue stack realistic, and does it model a narrowing arbitrage spread rather than assuming today's continues?

Is there a contracted element, tolling or floor, or is the project fully merchant and therefore equity-heavy?

Which EIB, EBRD, InvestEU or national support instruments can the project access, and is it structured to use them?

Is the equipment from a bankable manufacturer with regional service support?

Is there a credible EPC track record and a realistic construction and commissioning timeline?

Is there an insurance concept covering every phase, from transport to operation?

What is the grid connection status, export limit and queue position?

Does the model survive a conservative stress test, low spreads, delayed connection, one difficult year?

The bottom line

The technology works. The economics can be modelled. What determines bankability in Southeast Europe in 2026 is whether the project remains financeable and insurable once contract structures, revenue paths, cannibalisation, delivery risk and the regulatory environment are all stress-tested together, under realistic assumptions.

The market has moved past the phase where installed megawatts were the headline. The projects that reach financial close now are the ones that treat bankability not as a box to tick at the end, but as the discipline that shapes the project from the first day.
