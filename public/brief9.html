Issue No 09 Southeast Europe · Solar and Storage Intelligence 11 November 2026
Brief No 08 found the peak price below the whole-day average on three exchanges at once and called it the shape of the market. October kept the inversion on all three and almost closed it, while the level rose by between 40 and 56 per cent in a single month. Two months, three exchanges, two different answers to the same question. The discount was never the number worth modelling.
01 In FocusRegion

The discount shrank. The level did not.

Three Southeast European exchanges published October 2026 monthly averages for both a whole-day and a peak block. On all three the peak average again cleared below the whole-day average, as it did in September. On all three the gap narrowed sharply.

IBEX in Bulgaria printed a day-ahead baseload average of EUR 232.03/MWh against a peak average of EUR 217.66/MWh for October, on 2.915 TWh traded. SEEPEX in Serbia printed EUR 234.31/MWh baseload against a euro-peak average of EUR 229.17/MWh, on 579 308 MWh. The North Macedonian day-ahead exchange printed an average market-clearing price of EUR 224.74/MWh against a peak-price average of EUR 210.90/MWh, on 186 900 MWh. Each of those figures is reported by Serbia Energy from the exchanges' own October statistics, dated 09.11.2026 for Bulgaria and North Macedonia and 03.11.2026 for Serbia.

The gaps that follow from those published pairs are 6.2 per cent on IBEX, 2.2 per cent on SEEPEX and 6.2 per cent on the Macedonian exchange. Those three percentages are arithmetic on the published averages, not figures any exchange published. In September the same calculation on the figures in Brief No 08 gave 23.3 per cent, 12.1 per cent and 19.3 per cent.

So the direction held and the magnitude collapsed. A revenue model carrying a fixed percentage haircut against baseload would have been wrong in both months, and wrong in opposite directions: far too generous in September, far too harsh in October.

What moved was the level. Bulgaria's baseload average rose about 40 per cent from EUR 166.24/MWh in September. Serbia's rose 56.2 per cent and its euro-peak average rose 73.8 per cent, which is why the gap nearly closed: the peak block rose faster than the day. North Macedonia's average rose 49.3 per cent. Romania, which publishes a weighted average rather than a baseload and peak pair, averaged EUR 239.32/MWh on OPCOM in October on about 1.263 TWh traded, on a report of 09.11.2026 that does not state whether that average is baseload or weighted.

One number published this month shows how far both proxies sit from the thing they stand in for. Reporting ACER's 2026 market integration monitoring on 04.11.2026, Balkan Green Energy News gives the Greek solar capture price for May 2026 as EUR 24.63 against an average market price of EUR 88.95, attributed to DAPEEP. That is a capture price proper, output-weighted against settlement prices, and it is roughly a quarter of the market average for the same month. Neither a baseload average nor a peak block comes close to describing it.

My position. I am inclined to think the useful conclusion from two consecutive months is not a revised discount but the abandonment of the discount as an input. The shape of the day is what changed, and the only inputs that survive a change of shape are the hourly generation profile of the specific plant, hourly settlement prices rather than monthly averages, and whatever is on site that can move energy between hours.

06 Elevat AnglePosition

One clarification first, on our own text. Brief No 08 printed the Tenevo complex at 311 MW / 773 MWh of storage across three battery phases, brought into commercial operation at the inauguration at the start of October. The EBRD announcement of 02.11.2026 describes the site differently: an existing 242 MWp PV plant with one 65 MW / 260 MWh battery, plus a second battery of 246 MW / 512.5 MWh that it is now financing. Two systems, not three phases. The power adds to the same 311 MW and the energy to 772.5 MWh against the 773 MWh we printed. What was not visible last month is that the larger of the two reached financial close only in November. I do not think the earlier line was wrong on the information then available. I do think it shows how quickly an inauguration and a financing can be read as one event when they are a month apart.

On the discount, I would now stop using it. Two months, three exchanges, and two answers that differ by between three and five and a half times. September gave 23.3, 19.3 and 12.1 per cent below the whole-day average. October gives 6.2, 6.2 and 2.2 per cent, on my own arithmetic from the published pairs, so the gap narrowed by a factor of 3.8 in Bulgaria, 3.1 in North Macedonia and 5.5 in Serbia. A fixed haircut would have overstated revenue in one month and understated it in the next. The published Greek capture price of EUR 24.63 for May 2026 against a market average of EUR 88.95 says the same thing from the other direction: where the real number is published, it sits nowhere near either block average. The haircut was always a convenience. It is now a measurably wrong one.

What I would take forward is the direction of the Bulgarian series, not a ratio. ESO's own figures run 3.43 GW before the end of May, 5.41 GW in mid August and 6.23 GW in late October. Two in-window reports put Bulgarian solar at about 7.2 and about 7.3 GW, neither attributed and neither dated, so I am not going to build a parity argument on a denominator I cannot source. The battery series alone says enough: a system that added roughly 2.8 GW of battery power in five months is being rebuilt around the shape of the day, and the question for an operating park stops being what the annual average price will be and becomes which hours it can still sell into and what it owns that can move energy out of the others.

And the queues keep answering the same way. Bosnia and Herzegovina carries 18.1 GW of solar and wind applications against 5 547 MW installed, with its own system operator saying the grid is not ready. Kosovo has a lowest wind bid of EUR 75.94/MWh on the table, not yet awarded, in a system that has lost a thermal unit for the winter. Albania is preparing 300 MW of solar with mandatory co-located storage while its grid capacity is still being assessed. Three markets, three stages, one constraint. An existing connection with something on it that can shift hours is still the asset worth owning in this region, and nothing in October changed that.
