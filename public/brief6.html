Danube hits record low — SEE grid on emergency footing

The Danube reached 10 cm in Budapest on August 4 — well below the previous all-time low of 33 cm set in 2018. Serbia's Djerdap I and II hydro plants operate at 20–30% capacity. Romania shut down one reactor at Cernavodă and deployed its navy to blast rock to redirect cooling water. Bulgaria warned of potential Kozloduy curtailment. Solar and BESS are the only generation assets unaffected by the drought — and suddenly the most valuable on the grid.

05 — Policy Watch · Regulatory developments July–August 2026
🇷🇴 Romania — ENERGY EMERGENCY
State of energy alert declared — Danube at 1,500 m³/s (⅓ of July average). Cernavodă nuclear at 50% capacity, one reactor shut down. Romanian navy carried out controlled underwater explosions near Bala Canal to redirect cooling water. Acting PM Bolojan called on citizens to reduce consumption in peak hours. Carmakers Dacia and Ford halted production until Aug 19. Day-ahead prices hit €340/MWh on Aug 4. Grid capacity auctions (Oct 30) remain on schedule — Transelectrica has confirmed no delays.
🇷🇸 Serbia — ENERGY EMERGENCY
Djerdap I + II at 20–30% capacity. PM Macut presided over emergency energy meeting Aug 4. Citizens asked to limit AC and large appliances. Danube level nearing 1909 negative record at Serbia entry point. Emergency declared in three municipalities. SEEPEX settled at €107/MWh — but Serbia is importing, not exporting.
🇧🇬 Bulgaria
Bulgaria net exporter during crisis — BESS enabling new role. Bulgarian solar output covers domestic demand and exports to Romania, Serbia, North Macedonia and Greece simultaneously. BESS fleet (4,800MW / 14GWh) imports cheap midday solar, exports in evening peaks. Kozloduy nuclear flagged as potential curtailment risk if Danube falls further. Bulgaria's RESTORE deadline was July 31, 2026 — projects commissioned by deadline confirmed.
🇬🇷 Greece
Curtailments 2026 forecast: 3.3–3.7 TWh (+75% vs 2025). Greece relies on Bulgarian imports during Danube crisis — ironic given Greece's own curtailment problem. Curtailments already represent 12% of renewables output in 2026. Remote control law for RES >400kW in force. No BESS financing solution in sight.
🇲🇰 North Macedonia
Ministry launches first standalone BESS grant scheme. Public call issued July–August 2026. Štip wind farm (Končar + Dalekovod) construction started — first phase. Intraday market operational since May 6.

EuroHPC €10B AI Gigafactories call — energy demand signal. Invitation to bids for AI compute infrastructure published Aug 4. Each Gigafactory requires 100–500MW of firm power. 06 — Elevat Angle

The Danube crisis is the most important stress test SEE's energy infrastructure has faced in a decade. Record-low water at 10 cm in Budapest. Serbia's largest hydro plants at 20% capacity. Romania deploying its navy to blast riverbeds. Bulgaria's nuclear plant on watch. And in the middle of all this — solar panels keep generating, batteries keep charging and discharging, and Bulgaria emerges as a net exporter to the region. This is not a coincidence. It is the structural argument for the energy transition made visible in real time.

Bulgaria's BESS fleet — 4,800 MW / 14+ GWh — has changed the country's grid role. In the old model, Bulgaria exported baseload coal power. In the new model, it imports cheap midday solar from across the region, stores it, and re-exports it in evening peaks when neighbours are desperate for supply. Romania was paying €340/MWh on August 4. Bulgaria's batteries were buying at near-zero and selling into that spread. The business model works. The infrastructure investment thesis is validated.

Greece remains the cautionary tale. 13 GW of solar installed, 3.3–3.7 TWh forecast to be curtailed in 2026, zero operational BESS from 900 MW tendered, and now relying on Bulgarian imports during the regional crisis. The country that built solar fastest is least prepared to benefit from it. The connection between curtailment, BESS deployment failure, and import dependency is not accidental — it is the predictable result of a financing framework that never resolved the bankability of storage.

The drought exposes a structural vulnerability that the energy transition was supposed to solve. Hydro was supposed to be the flexibility provider. At 10 cm of Danube, it provides nothing. The lesson for the next decade of infrastructure planning in SEE is that flexibility cannot depend on a single resource — and that BESS is not optional for any grid with serious renewable penetration. The countries that understood this early (Bulgaria, Romania) are weathering the crisis. The countries that didn't (Serbia, Greece) are in emergency mode.
