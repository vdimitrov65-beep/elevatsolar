What Does an Owner's Engineer Do for Your Solar Project?

An Owner's Engineer is the independent set of eyes that verifies the EPC contractor's promises before, during and after construction.

Elevat Solar Consulting · Guide · October 2026

Utility-scale solar and BESS projects commit large amounts of capital on the strength of an EPC contractor's promises. An Owner's Engineer is the independent set of eyes that verifies those promises, before, during and after construction.

Why solar projects need independent technical oversight

A utility-scale PV plant is, on paper, a simple asset: modules, inverters, structure, cables, transformer. In practice, the gap between a good plant and a mediocre one is large, and it shows up in the energy yield, the maintenance costs and ultimately the return on investment.

The challenge is structural. The EPC contractor who builds your plant also designed it, selected the equipment and will certify that it meets the contractual performance ratio. The contractor has every reason to optimise for build cost rather than for 25-year energy output. That misalignment is not adversarial. It is simply how incentives work in fixed-price EPC contracts.

An Owner's Engineer (OE) represents the asset owner's technical interests, independently of the EPC contractor. The OE reviews the design, audits equipment procurement, monitors construction quality and verifies that the finished plant meets its specifications. It is the technical counterpart of what legal counsel does in contract negotiation, except that the OE's arena is string sizing, cable routing, tracker alignment and inverter commissioning.

Key distinction. An Owner's Engineer works for the asset owner or developer. An Independent Engineer (IE), sometimes called a Lender's Technical Advisor (LTA), works for the financing bank. Their scopes overlap, but their client and their perspective are different. A project typically needs both.

What an Owner's Engineer actually does

The scope of an OE engagement varies with the project stage. Below is a walkthrough of the core deliverables across the lifecycle of a utility-scale solar plant.

1. Design review

Before construction begins, the OE reviews the EPC contractor's detailed design package. This is where the highest-leverage corrections happen. A design review typically covers:

  • String sizing. Verifying that open-circuit voltage at minimum site temperature stays within inverter limits, and that the MPP voltage window at maximum operating temperature is within the inverter's tracking range. Getting this wrong costs energy every day the plant operates.
  • DC/AC ratio. Confirming that the chosen overplanting ratio is justified by the site's irradiation profile and curtailment regime, and not simply set by habit.
  • Cable sizing. Checking conductor cross-sections against three simultaneous criteria: ampacity at installation temperature, voltage drop against a stated target, and short-circuit thermal withstand.
  • Electrical protection. String fuse sizing, circuit breaker coordination, SPD placement, grounding and equipotential bonding.
  • Structural adequacy. Foundation design against site-specific wind, snow and soil data; tracker torque tube and purlin capacity.
  • Grid compliance. Verifying that the plant meets the applicable network code: EU RfG type classification, local grid operator requirements and protection relay settings.

2. Equipment procurement support

The OE reviews equipment specifications and datasheets, assesses the bankability of the selected manufacturers and may conduct factory acceptance testing (FAT). On a large plant, differences in real-world degradation between module suppliers compound over the whole life of the asset.

3. Construction monitoring

During construction, the OE conducts periodic or continuous site inspections. The focus is on workmanship that affects long-term performance and safety: module installation torque, cable management, grounding connections, tracker alignment and trenching depth. Defects caught during construction cost a fraction of what they cost after commissioning.

4. Commissioning and performance testing

At the end of construction, the OE witnesses commissioning tests, reviews the EPC contractor's as-built documentation and independently verifies that the plant meets its contractual performance ratio. This typically includes:

  • IV curve tracing on a sample of strings
  • Thermal imaging to detect cell-level defects
  • Insulation resistance testing of DC circuits
  • Grid protection relay testing against type-test certificates
  • Provisional acceptance testing (PAT) over a defined measurement period

5. Yield assessment validation

The OE independently reviews the energy yield assessment, typically produced by a third party, and validates its assumptions: meteorological data sources, transposition model, shading analysis, loss chain and degradation rates. On BESS-coupled plants, the OE also reviews the storage dispatch model and the round-trip efficiency assumptions.

Practical tip. If you are an asset owner financing the project with debt, you will need both an OE, who protects your equity, and an IE, who satisfies the lender's due diligence. The OE typically starts earlier and stays longer.

When do you need an Owner's Engineer?

The short answer: from the moment you sign a letter of intent with an EPC contractor. The longer answer depends on the project structure.

  • Pure equity or balance-sheet projects. The OE is your primary technical safeguard. Without a lender's IE, the OE is the only independent check on the EPC contractor's work.
  • Project-financed assets. The lender will appoint an IE, but the IE's scope is narrower and milestone-driven. The OE fills the gaps: continuous oversight, design input before the IE's first review, and post-COD operational support.
  • Acquisitions. A technical due diligence is effectively a compressed OE engagement. The same skill set applies: reviewing the design, assessing equipment condition, validating the yield and identifying latent risks.
  • Operational assets. If the plant underperforms, an OE can diagnose whether the issue is design-related (undersized cables, suboptimal string configuration), equipment-related (inverter firmware, module degradation) or operational (soiling, tracker misalignment).

What goes wrong without an Owner's Engineer

The failure patterns that independent review is meant to catch repeat across markets. Three of the most common:

Design shortcuts that erode yield. String lengths are set against standard test conditions instead of the site's minimum winter temperature. On cold mornings the open-circuit voltage can then exceed the inverter's maximum input voltage, and the plant loses energy or risks damage every winter for the life of the asset.

Procurement substitutions that create long-term risk. A contractor replaces the specified module with a cheaper alternative mid-procurement, citing supply delays. If nobody checks the replacement's construction and its PID test certificate under IEC 62804, a degradation risk enters the plant unnoticed and may only appear years later.

Construction deviations that surface after the warranty. Piles are driven shallower than the geotechnical recommendation to recover a compressed schedule. Without an independent inspector on site, the deviation is not recorded, and the consequences can appear after the defects liability period has expired, when they are entirely the owner's problem.

How to choose the right Owner's Engineer

Not all OE firms are equally useful. Here is what to look for.

  • Track record in your technology and market. An OE who has worked mostly on rooftop projects may not have the depth for a large ground-mount plant with single-axis trackers and co-located BESS. Grid codes also differ substantially between Western Europe, the Balkans and the Middle East, so regional experience matters.
  • Independence from the EPC contractor. The OE must have no commercial relationship with the EPC contractor, the module supplier or the inverter manufacturer. Any overlap creates a conflict of interest that defeats the purpose of the engagement.
  • Willingness to be on site. Desktop reviews are necessary but not sufficient. An OE who does not visit the site during construction is reviewing paperwork, not reality.
  • Clarity on deliverables. A good OE defines upfront what they will deliver: design review reports, inspection protocols, punch lists, commissioning witness reports and a final OE certificate. Vague scope leads to vague accountability.
  • Proportionality. An OE engagement should be sized to the project. A small rooftop plant does not need the same depth as a large utility-scale plant. Avoid both over-engineering, with reporting that slows construction, and under-engineering, with a single site visit and a rubber-stamp letter.

The bottom line

Owner's engineering is not an overhead cost. It is a risk management tool that pays for itself by catching design errors, procurement shortcuts and construction defects that would otherwise become the asset owner's problem for 25 years.

A single uncaught design error, such as a wrong string length, an undersized cable or a missed protection coordination issue, can cost more over the plant's lifetime than the whole OE engagement.

If you are deploying capital into solar or BESS, an independent technical partner is not optional. It is the difference between an asset that performs as modelled and one that underperforms from day one.
